01Context

A few decades ago, a car without a driver sounded absurd. Today, autonomous and talking systems are leaving our homes and moving onto our streets. Most of them are designed to inform, deliver or watch. Few are designed for what happens between the people around them.

Things we would never have imagined, already here:

  • 2011, 2014

    Talking to machines at home. Siri and Alexa made talking to an object normal indoors. Wetterbank® asks what happens when that moves outdoors, among strangers.

  • 2014

    Benches already got smart. Boston put solar-powered Soofa benches in parks to charge phones. Benches learned to charge your phone, but not to say hello.

    City of Boston

  • 2019

    A talking object at a station. Deutsche Bahn tested SEMMI, a service robot at Berlin Hauptbahnhof that answered questions, made small talk and told jokes, in several languages.

    leinetal24, June 2019

  • 2020–2025

    A self-driving bus next to Düsseldorf. Monheim am Rhein ran five autonomous minibuses on a regular route (A01). They were suspended in September 2025 when the manufacturer stopped supporting them. Such technology can arrive, and disappear again.

    Urban Transport Magazine

  • 2025

    A talking bus stop. Hamburg’s transport association presented a prototype AI stop that answers travellers’ questions in many languages, with a camera that can alert the police. The industry version of a talking stop is about information and safety, not about the people waiting.

    Notebookcheck

  • 2025

    Robots on the pavement. Starship robots deliver REWE groceries in Hamburg-Barmbek.

    Retail Technology Innovation Hub

  • 2026

    Cars without drivers. Waymo runs paid, fully driverless rides in 14 US cities.

    Drive Tesla Canada, September 2026

And the weather, the one thing everyone at a German tram stop has in common:

  • 62% of Germans talk about the weather several times a week or more (YouGov, 2026).
  • 70% see the weather as an acceptable small-talk topic, more than any other (Babbel / YouGov, 2025).
  • 48.5% of 18 to 24-year-olds find small talk uncomfortable (same survey).
  • 18 to 29-year-olds carry the highest loneliness burden in Germany (Einsamkeitsbarometer 2024).

The opener is easy. The step after it is hard. Wetterbank® is built for that step.

02The fiction

Wetterbank® is a bench at a tram stop that starts conversations between strangers, by helping them file complaints about the weather. It has three modes, each one step further from the safe topic: Wetter opens with a remark about the weather, Meckern takes complaints and forwards them, Namen drops the weather and asks for a name.

It is a design fiction: an object built to provoke a question rather than to be sold. As Near Future Laboratory put it about their own work, a design fiction “makes implications without making predictions”. Wetterbank® does not claim benches will talk. It asks what it would mean if they did.

The bureaucratic humour is deliberate. A machine that patiently files your complaint about drizzle plays to what machines are good at, endless patience for small, boring conversations, instead of pretending to be a person. The joke is the way in; the question is the point.

Wetterbank® began as a design fiction on my portfolio, with a fictional company, Wetterbank® GmbH, “founded 2031 after a 40-minute tram delay in which nobody said a word”. This site is where it continues as research.

03The question

How do interactive and autonomous systems shape social encounters in public space?

  1. Want. Do people even want to talk, to each other, or to a bench?

    Not everyone at a tram stop wants company. Silence in public is often a choice, not a problem.

  2. Help. Can a bench help an encounter, or does it get in the way?

    A bench that speaks first might lower the barrier, or do the awkward part for us, so that we never learn to.

  3. Future. How will we react when objects like this become normal, and should they?

    Driverless cars and talking stops show how quickly the unimaginable becomes infrastructure. The Monheim buses show it can also disappear again.

Two results, not one. Talking to the bench is not the same as talking to each other because of the bench. Every study below records both, and compares them with an ordinary bench.

04Field studies

From cheap to real. Each one is logged here when it happens, with what was real and what was staged.

Field study #001Planned

Talking to people at tram stops

Short conversations at Düsseldorf tram stops: how do you start talking to a stranger? Do you complain about the weather? Would you want a bench to start?

Real
Real people, real stops. No bench.
Answers
Question 1, and whether “Meckern” is a habit or a stereotype.
Field study #002Planned

Interviewing the bench

A speculative conversation with Wetterbank® itself, written and published as a transcript, after Reddy et al.’s “Making Everyday Things Talk” (2021).

Staged
Entirely. The bench is played by me.
Answers
What the bench would say, and what it should never say.
Field study #003Planned

Becoming the bench

A performer plays the bench with deliberately limited senses and a fixed script, after the techno-mimesis method of Dörrenbächer, Löffler and Hassenzahl (2020): not a person pretending to be a bench, but a person finding out what being a bench is like.

Staged
The bench. The questions it raises are real.
Answers
What a bench can notice, and when it should stay quiet.
Field study #004Planned

The staged tram stop

Actors play the bench and the strangers at a made-up tram stop. The audience watches, then discusses what worked, what fell flat, and what a bench should never say.

Staged
The stop, the bench, the strangers. The audience is real.
Answers
Questions 2 and 3, before any hardware exists.
Field study #005Planned

The ordinary bench

A visit to a “Plauderbank”: an ordinary bench with a sign saying you are happy to chat (for example in Wesel, NRW). If a sign already works, what would a talking bench add, or take away?

Real
Real bench, real people. No technology.
Answers
The baseline every other study is compared with.
Field study #006Planned

A real bench, a hidden voice

A bench at a real stop with a small speaker. The “system” is a person nearby choosing lines from the script, a Wizard-of-Oz study after Cornell Tech’s trash barrel robots. No recordings of faces or voices; everyone is told afterwards what was going on.

Real
The stop and the people.
Staged
The bench’s intelligence.
Answers
Does anyone get past the weather, and was it their idea or the bench’s?

05Precedents

What came before, grouped by what Wetterbank® takes from it.

Lineage

Research Wetterbank® directly builds on.

  • Mediating Urban Social Encounters: Co-Design of Robotic Street Furniture with Adolescents

    Dörrenbächer et al., CHI 2026

    Takes: street furniture as a mediator between people, explored through theatre; Wetterbank® adds adults, a tram stop, and the weather as the opener.

  • When park benches bring people together

    ENACT, Uni Siegen with FFT Düsseldorf, 2026

    Takes: the question of which social roles technology could take on in public space, and speculative role-play as a method.

  • Becoming a Robot: Overcoming Anthropomorphism with Techno-Mimesis

    Dörrenbächer, Löffler & Hassenzahl, CHI 2020

    Takes: the method for field study #003.

  • Otherware

    Hassenzahl et al., 2020–2021

    Takes: design machines as counterparts with their own strengths, not as fake humans.

Talking public objects

Proof that people talk to street furniture, and what can go wrong.

  • Hello Lamp Post

    PAN Studio with Tom Armitage, Bristol, 2013

    Takes: people text lamp posts and post boxes, and the objects pass on earlier answers. Object talk that turns into an exchange between people.

  • Talking Statues

    Sing London, London and Manchester, 2014

    Takes: scripted, funny character voices written and performed by writers and actors. Also what Wetterbank® is not: these statues talk at you, not between strangers.

  • Malmö’s talking bins

    City of Malmö, 2017

    Takes: a warning. When the joke is the whole interaction, it wears off.

  • Talking Treebot

    CityLAB Berlin

    Takes: a tree whose conversational mood follows real weather data. A close cousin of a weather-aware bench.

Low-tech baseline

Getting strangers to talk without any technology.

  • Happy to Chat benches

    Allison Owen-Jones, Cardiff, 2019

    Takes: a sign on a bench is enough to invite conversation. The comparison for question 2.

  • Plauderbänke

    Wesel (NRW), Heidelberg and other German towns, 2023–

    Takes: the same idea, close to Düsseldorf. The site for field study #005.

  • Modified Social Benches

    Jeppe Hein, since 2005

    Takes: the shape of a bench already changes how people sit together, before it says a word.

Method

How to test a talking object with real people.

  • Trash barrel robots

    Fanjun Bu, Wendy Ju and colleagues, Cornell Tech, New York, 2022–2023

    Takes: Wizard-of-Oz robots in public space, with interviews afterwards. The template for field study #006.

  • Making Everyday Things Talk

    Reddy et al., CHI 2021

    Takes: speculative conversations with objects as a research method. The template for field study #002.

  • Remote X

    Rimini Protokoll, Berlin, 2013

    Takes: an artificial voice that guides a group of strangers through a city. Theatre as a way to test what a machine voice does to people.

Tone

Deadpan fiction that is taken seriously.

  • The Bureau of Linguistical Reality

    Alicia Escott & Heidi Quante, since 2014

    Takes: a fictional public office run as participatory research, with numbered field studies.

  • TBD Catalog

    Near Future Laboratory, 2014

    Takes: the ordinary, everyday near future, told completely straight.

  • Uninvited Guests

    Superflux, 2015

    Takes: funny, humane criticism of autonomous objects, and keeping the object deliberately simple.
